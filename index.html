From synapses to neural populations.
From milliseconds to days.

How does our brain compute memory?

I am currently a postdoctoral research scientist at Columbia University's Mortimer B. Zuckerman Mind Brain Behavior Institute, where I am co-mentored by Drs. Attila Losonczy and Stefano Fusi. My long-term goal is to uncover the principles of memory computation in the brain by understanding how memories are encoded, maintained, and updated across timescales ranging from milliseconds to days. To address these questions across biological scales—from synapses to neural populations—I combine in vivo cellular- and synapse-resolved imaging in behaving animals with genetic and molecular perturbations and computational modeling.

Before joining Columbia University, I earned my B.S. in Biological Sciences and Ph.D. in Medical Science and Engineering from the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST). During my graduate training, co-mentored by Drs. Pilhan Kim and Doyun Lee, I developed customized intravital microscopy systems for deep-tissue imaging and investigated how the hippocampus supports associative social memory.

Research

1. Stable and Flexible Hippocampal Representations I investigate how hippocampal circuits generate stable yet flexible neural representations that support memory across diverse behavioral contexts. During my doctoral training, I developed a head-fixed social interaction paradigm combined with in vivo two-photon imaging to demonstrate how hippocampal CA1 neurons encode social identity and reward expectancy during associative social memory. More recently, I have shown that recurrent connectivity along the proximodistal axis of hippocampal CA3 shapes the geometry, stability, and adaptability of spatial representations, revealing local circuit mechanisms that support flexible memory computation.

2. Synaptic Computation of Memory My current research, supported by the NIH through the K99/R00 Pathway to Independence Award, seeks to uncover the synaptic mechanisms that generate hippocampal representations of episodic memory. I investigate how synaptic inputs are integrated to give rise to these representations, how synaptic integration rules differ across dendritic compartments within individual neurons, and how they vary across hippocampal cell types defined by their dendritic morphology. To address these questions, I combine in vivo spine-resolved functional imaging in behaving animals with genetically encoded neurotransmitter sensors and computational modeling.

3. Advanced In Vivo Imaging Technologies Throughout my graduate and postdoctoral training, I have developed and applied advanced optical imaging technologies to investigate neural computation across biological scales, from individual synapses to neural populations. During my doctoral training, I built customized intravital imaging platforms, including side-view confocal endomicroscopy for deep-tissue cellular imaging in living animals. During my postdoctoral training, I contributed to the development of high-speed three-photon microscopy for optical recording of neural activity in deep hippocampal regions of behaving animals. In ongoing projects, I apply 3D random-access two-photon microscopy to achieve large-volume, cellular-level functional imaging and synapse-resolved imaging of dendritic spines and axonal boutons in behaving animals.
